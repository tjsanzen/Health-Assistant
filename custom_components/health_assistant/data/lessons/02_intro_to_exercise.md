# Intro to Exercise

In the previous lesson, Intro to Nutrition, we talked about how there are two variables we can control when managing our weight: calories in and calories out. Nutrition covers the calories in portion, and so exercise helps us cover the calories out portion. However, exercise is important far beyond just weight management.

One of the simplest forms of exercise, walking, has been scientifically associated with lower risks of heart disease, heart attack, dementia, falls, and mortality in general.

That said, if you are not someone who regularly exercises, or have not in a while, it can feel really intimidating to start. This guide will walk you through choosing the best exercises for your body and circumstances.

---

## What is the best exercise for me?

Let's start with the question everyone really wants the answer to, and the answer is simple, albeit not satisfying: the best exercise for you is the one that you can do consistently.

A "perfect" program you quit after three weeks loses to an "okay" program you're still doing a year from now. So when you're choosing, ask yourself:

- **Do I actually enjoy this, or at least not dread it?**
- **Can I fit it into my real schedule,** not my ideal schedule?
- **Do I have access to what it requires** (a gym, equipment, a safe place to walk)?
- **Does it suit my body right now,** including any injuries or limitations?

### What the guidelines recommend

Global health guidelines give a useful target to work toward. The World Health Organization recommends that adults get:

- **150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity** aerobic activity per week, **or** 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent mix of the two
- **Muscle-strengthening activity on 2 or more days per week,** working all the major muscle groups

The WHO's motto here is that every move counts, and the guidelines stress starting small and building up gradually. If you're currently doing nothing, anything you add is a win, and you don't have to hit those numbers on day one.

> **How do I know if it's moderate or vigorous?** Use the **talk test.** At moderate intensity, you can hold a conversation but couldn't sing. At vigorous intensity, you can only manage a few words at a time before needing a breath.

## Resistance Training

Resistance training (also called strength training or weight training) means making your muscles work against a load, whether that's dumbbells, machines, resistance bands, or your own bodyweight.

### Why it matters

- **Preserves and builds muscle,** which is especially important if you're eating in a calorie deficit, since your body will otherwise pull from muscle as well as fat
- **Strengthens bones and joints**
- **Raises your capability for everyday life:** carrying groceries, climbing stairs, getting up off the floor
- **Is specifically recommended in the guidelines,** separate from cardio, because it provides benefits cardio doesn't

### How to start

Beginners generally do best with a **full-body routine 2 to 3 days per week,** with at least a day of rest between sessions. Build the routine around a handful of basic movement patterns:

| Movement | Example exercises |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| Squat / leg push | Bodyweight squat, goblet squat, leg press |
| Hinge | Romanian deadlift, hip thrust, glute bridge |
| Push | Push-up (wall, incline, or floor), dumbbell press |
| Pull | Dumbbell row, band row, lat pulldown |
| Carry / core | Farmer's carry, plank, dead bug |

### The key principle: progressive overload

Your body only adapts if you give it a reason to. **Progressive overload** just means gradually asking a bit more of your muscles over time: a few more reps, a little more weight, an extra set, or slower and more controlled movement. Writing your workouts down makes this much easier, since you can see what you did last time and try to beat it slightly.

A common starting point is **2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 repetitions** per exercise, using a weight that feels challenging by the last couple of reps while still letting you keep good form. Form comes before weight.

### Do I need a gym?

No. Bodyweight exercises, resistance bands, and a pair of adjustable dumbbells will take most beginners a long way. A gym adds variety and heavier loads, which becomes more useful as you progress.

## Cardio

Cardio (cardiovascular or aerobic exercise) is anything that raises your heart rate and keeps it up for a sustained period: walking, jogging, cycling, swimming, rowing, dancing, hiking, and so on.

### Start with walking

Walking is underrated. It's low-impact, free, needs no equipment, and the evidence for it is strong. A 2025 review in *The Lancet Public Health* pooled dozens of studies and found that people averaging about **7,000 steps per day** had roughly a **47% lower risk of death from any cause** than people at about 2,000 steps. The review also found lower risks of heart disease, dementia, and type 2 diabetes. Even about **4,000 steps** per day was linked to meaningfully better outcomes than 2,000.

A few caveats worth knowing:

- These are observational studies, which show an *association*. Healthier people also tend to walk more, so some of the effect may be reverse causation.
- Benefits level off for many outcomes around 5,000 to 7,000 steps. The popular 10,000 target can still be a good goal for people who are more active, but it isn't a magic threshold.

So if you're starting from a low baseline, **don't aim for 10,000 steps tomorrow.** Find your current average and add 1,000 or so steps to it.

### Types of cardio

| Type | What it is | Good for |
| ----------- | ----------- | ----------- |
| **Low-intensity steady state (LISS)** | Walking, easy cycling, easy swimming at a pace where you can chat | Beginners, recovery days, building a base, long sessions |
| **Moderate-intensity** | Brisk walking, steady jogging, a cycling pace that gets you breathing harder | Hitting the weekly guideline, general fitness |
| **High-intensity intervals (HIIT)** | Short bursts of hard effort alternating with rest | Time-efficient fitness gains; best added gradually once you have a base |

You don't need to do all three. For most people, a mix of regular walking plus one or two more demanding sessions per week covers the bases.

### Cardio and calories

Remember the point from the Intro to Nutrition lesson: exercise burns fewer calories than most people expect, and it's easy to eat those calories back. That doesn't make cardio pointless. It just means it works best as one piece of the puzzle alongside nutrition, not as a way to "earn" or cancel out food.

## Putting It Together

If you're starting from zero, here's a gentle on-ramp:

1. **Weeks 1–2:** Walk most days and do 2 short full-body resistance sessions (20–30 minutes).
2. **Weeks 3–4:** Add a few minutes to your walks and a set or two to your strength workouts.
3. **Beyond:** Gradually work toward the guideline targets, adjusting based on how you feel and what you enjoy.

Soreness in the first week or two is normal. Sharp pain, dizziness, chest pain, or anything that feels wrong is not. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant or postpartum, or haven't been active in a long time, check with a doctor before ramping up.
