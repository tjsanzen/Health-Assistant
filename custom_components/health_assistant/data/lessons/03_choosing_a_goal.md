# What should my health goals be?

There are many different health goals that different people have. Some come in with a pre-defined and decided goal to lose weight. Others may need to gain weight, and still others may not care about their weight at all, but want to improve strength, fitness, or just overall health.

Whatever your goal is, your journey is yours alone, and choosing an option that works for you is the most important thing.

This lesson won't tell you what you *should* want. It will give you a way to think about each path so you can pick one on purpose, rather than by default.

---

## Should I lose weight?

Losing weight is the most common goal, but it isn't automatically the right one for everyone. Some questions to ask yourself:

- **Is my current weight affecting how I feel or function?** Joint pain, low energy, difficulty with everyday activity, or breathlessness can be signs that carrying less weight might help.
- **Is there a medical reason?** A doctor may recommend weight loss for blood pressure, blood sugar, or other health markers. If so, work with them on the specifics.
- **Is this goal about my health, or only about a number or appearance?** Wanting to look different is a valid reason, but it's worth knowing which one is driving you, since the "number" can be a frustrating target (more on that below).

> **A note on BMI:** BMI (body mass index) is a quick screening number, not a verdict. It can't tell the difference between muscle and fat, and it doesn't show where weight is carried. Waist measurement, strength, fitness, and health markers like blood pressure and blood work give a fuller picture.

### How fast is reasonable?

Slow and steady wins here. As covered in Intro to Nutrition, it's generally not recommended to run a deficit of more than 500 to 700 calories per day, especially if you're trying to keep or build muscle at the same time. For most people, a pace of roughly **0.5 to 1% of body weight per week** is a sensible ceiling, and slower is perfectly fine.

### Protecting your muscle

When you eat in a deficit, you want the weight you lose to be mostly fat, not muscle. Two things help most:

1. **Eat enough protein** (see Intro to Nutrition for your target)
2. **Do resistance training** (see Intro to Exercise)

### When to be careful

Weight loss isn't a good fit for everyone. If you're already at or below a healthy weight, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have a history of disordered eating, talk with a doctor or registered dietitian before restricting calories. And if tracking food or weight ever starts to feel obsessive, stressful, or distressing, that's a signal to step back and get support, not to push harder.

## Should I gain weight?

Gaining weight is a legitimate health goal too. It might make sense if you're underweight, recovering from illness or injury, or building muscle for strength or sport.

### Gaining well

The goal for most people isn't just "more weight," but **more muscle with a modest amount of fat**. To do that:

- **Eat in a small surplus.** As covered in Intro to Nutrition, that's about 500 calories above your maintenance of roughly {mxcal} per day. Smaller surpluses tend to add less fat along the way.
- **Lift weights.** Without resistance training giving your body a reason to build muscle, a surplus mostly turns into fat.
- **Prioritize protein** (see Intro to Nutrition for your target).
- **Be patient.** Building muscle is slow. A gain of roughly 0.25 to 0.5 pounds per week is a reasonable expectation for many people, and beginners can sometimes gain a bit faster.

### If gaining is hard

Some people struggle to eat enough. Calorie-dense foods (nuts, nut butters, olive oil, dried fruit, whole milk or yogurt, rice) and liquid calories like smoothies can make it easier. If you're underweight without meaning to be, or you've lost weight unintentionally, see a doctor to check for an underlying cause.

## What if I don't care about my weight at all?

Totally valid. Plenty of people have goals that have nothing to do with the scale. Some examples:

| Goal | What it might look like |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| **Get stronger** | Track your lifts and aim to add weight or reps over time |
| **Build fitness** | Work toward walking farther, jogging a distance, or improving a time |
| **Improve daily energy and health** | Hit a step target, sleep more consistently, eat more whole foods |
| **Body recomposition** | Build muscle and lose fat at the same time, with the scale barely moving. This works best for beginners, people returning after a break, and those with higher body fat |
| **Maintain** | Stay where you are and build habits that last |

Goals like these have a bonus: they're about what your body can *do*, which tends to be more motivating than a number that bounces around from day to day.

## How to choose

If you're stuck, try this:

1. **Write down why.** What would be different in your life if you hit this goal? The answer tells you whether it's the right goal.
2. **Start with one primary goal.** Chasing weight loss, muscle gain, and a marathon at once usually means doing none of them well.
3. **Make it specific and measurable,** like "walk 7,000 steps most days" or "squat my bodyweight" rather than "get healthy."
4. **Set it up to be checked in on.** Pick a time, a month or two out, to look at your progress and adjust. Your goal is allowed to change.
5. **Aim for habits, not just outcomes.** You control what you do (eating, training, sleeping, walking). You don't fully control what the scale says on a given Tuesday.

As the Intro to Nutrition lesson put it, we shouldn't be chasing a specific number on a scale or a specific calorie count, or cutting out our favorite foods. The aim is full-body, holistic **health.**
