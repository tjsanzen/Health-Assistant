# Dietary Fats & Oils

Few topics in nutrition get argued about as loudly as fat. Butter vs. olive oil, tallow vs. canola, "seed oils are poison" vs. "seed oils are heart healthy." 

The short version: **the type of fat matters, but the overall pattern of what you eat matters more than which bottle sits next to your stove.** Cutting out fast food and greasy junk will make a much bigger difference than choosing butter or extra virgin olive oil for your breakfast fried egg. 

---

## Why Fat Matters

Fat is the most calorie-dense macronutrient, at about **9 calories per gram** (compared to 4 for protein and carbs). That's why a single tablespoon of cooking oil carries nearly 120 calories. It's also essential: fats support hormone production, brain health, and the absorption of vitamins A, D, E, and K.

So the question should never be "should I eat fat," but rather "which fats, and in place of what?"

That second part is the key to understanding most of the controversy. Almost every study on this topic is really asking what happens when you **swap** one thing for another. "Saturated fat is bad" and "seed oils are bad" can't both be fully true, because in most studies one is being swapped for the other.

## The Four Types of Fat

```
Dietary Fats
├── Unsaturated
│   ├── Monounsaturated (olive oil, avocado, almonds)
│   └── Polyunsaturated (salmon, walnuts, flax, most vegetable oils)
├── Saturated (butter, tallow, lard, fatty meat, full-fat dairy, coconut & palm oil)
└── Trans (partially hydrogenated oils; avoid)
```

### Saturated Fat

Found mainly in animal products (meat fat, butter, full-fat dairy) and tropical oils (coconut, palm).

**Research:**
- Saturated fat reliably raises **LDL cholesterol** compared to unsaturated fats.
- A large Cochrane review of 15 randomized trials (about 56,000 people) found that reducing saturated fat for at least two years lowered combined cardiovascular events (heart attacks, strokes, and similar) by about **17%**. The benefit came when saturated fat was replaced with **polyunsaturated fat or starchy foods**.
- The same review found **little or no effect on overall death rates**. Fewer heart events did not clearly translate into living longer.
- The effect is real but modest: roughly 56 people would need to cut saturated fat for about four years to prevent one cardiovascular event.

**Notes:**
- The food source seems to matter. Dairy and whole-food sources of saturated fat look more neutral in observational studies than processed meat or commercial baked goods.
- Butter and tallow are not the same as the saturated fat in, say, yogurt or a piece of steak. Butter, and beef fat to a lesser extent, raise LDL more than olive oil does.

**The guideline:** Keep saturated fat **under 10% of daily calories**. This limit was kept in the 2025–2030 US Dietary Guidelines, even though those guidelines also list butter and beef tallow as cooking options. Many nutrition researchers and cardiology groups pointed out that the two messages pull against each other, since a single tablespoon of butter (about 7 g of saturated fat) already uses up a large share of a day's budget.

> **Quick math:** On a 2,000-calorie day, 10% is 200 calories of saturated fat, or about **22 grams**.

### Monounsaturated Fat

Found in extra virgin olive oil, avocados, almonds, and other nuts. These show up consistently in observational research as part of healthy eating patterns, and olive oil performs better than butter in head-to-head comparisons.

One honest caveat: few randomized trials have directly tested swapping saturated fat for monounsaturated fat specifically, so the strongest trial evidence is for polyunsaturated fat, and the monounsaturated case rests more on observational data and eating-pattern research.

### Polyunsaturated Fat

This group includes **omega-3s** (salmon, mackerel, walnuts, flax, chia) and **omega-6s** (most vegetable and seed oils). Your body can't make either one, so you have to eat them.

The omega-3 side is straightforward and well regarded. The omega-6 side is where the "seed oil" debate lives.

### Trans Fat

The one with almost no controversy. Industrial trans fats (partially hydrogenated oils) raise LDL, lower HDL, and are strongly tied to heart disease. They were effectively banned from the US food supply. Tiny amounts occur naturally in beef and dairy, which is a much less worrying situation. Avoid the artificial kind entirely.

## The Seed Oil Debate

"Seed oils" usually means corn, soybean, canola, sunflower, and safflower oil. They're rich in linoleic acid, an omega-6 polyunsaturated fat.

### The case that they're fine or beneficial
- Major health organizations, including the American Heart Association, continue to recommend replacing saturated and trans fats with **liquid plant oils**.
- Large observational studies, including long-running Nurses' Health Study data, found that people with higher linoleic acid levels in their blood had **lower cardiovascular risk**.
- The popular claim that linoleic acid causes inflammation comes mostly from lab and animal studies. In humans, only a very small fraction (around 0.2%) of dietary linoleic acid converts to arachidonic acid, and that molecule has anti-inflammatory roles as well as inflammatory ones. Human evidence generally shows linoleic acid does **not** increase inflammation.
- The AHA recommends getting roughly 5–10% of daily calories from omega-6 fats.

### The case for caution
- Researcher Christopher Ramsden recovered long-unpublished data from two older randomized trials. In the **Sydney Diet Heart Study**, swapping saturated fat for safflower oil was associated with **more** deaths from heart disease and from all causes. In the **Minnesota Coronary Experiment**, the vegetable-oil diet lowered cholesterol but did not lower the risk of death.
- His argument is that incomplete publication of old trials made vegetable oils look more beneficial than the full data supports.

### How to weigh it
- The Minnesota trial was done in state mental hospitals and a nursing home, so how well it applies to the general public is debatable.
- The old trials used margarines that likely contained trans fats, which muddies the comparison.
- Other analyses of randomized trials found **no association** of omega-6 intake with heart disease, which points to *neutral* rather than *harmful*.
- **The big confounder:** seed oils are baked into huge amounts of ultra-processed food. When studies link them to poor health, it's hard to separate the oil from the fries, pastries, and packaged snacks it's in.

**Bottom line:** There's little support from human trials for the claim that seed oils are harmful, but the strongest "they're proven lifesavers" version isn't airtight either. For a home kitchen, they're a reasonable option, just not clearly better than olive oil.

## What About Cooking With Them?

For everyday home cooking, a few practical notes:

- **Extra virgin olive oil** is a solid default for sautéing and roasting. The worry that it breaks down under normal home cooking heat is mostly overblown.
- **Avocado oil or canola oil** are good neutral options for higher-heat searing or frying.
- **Butter, tallow, and bacon grease** are fine in small amounts for flavor. The concern is making them your default fat for most meals, every day.
- **A little oil on an egg once or twice a week is not a health decision.** The studies describe patterns over years. A teaspoon of butter in the pan barely dents a daily saturated fat budget.

### The Bigger Lever: Fried and Fast Food

Most people get the bulk of their seed oils and trans fats not from home cooking but from **deep-fried food, fast food, and packaged products**. That food is rarely just an oil problem. It's the whole package: refined carbs, lots of sodium, processed meat, big portions, and often oil that has been heated over and over. The American Heart Association has noted that most ultra-processed foods have poor nutritional quality.

There hasn't been a head-to-head trial of "tallow at home" versus "cutting fast food," so this ranking is an inference from how the evidence fits together. But it's a well-supported one: **the gap between reasonable fat choices at home is small compared to how often you eat out of a fryer or drive-through bag.**

## Where the Science Is Still Unsettled

It's worth being honest about what we don't know:

- Whether polyunsaturated fat beats saturated fat for **overall mortality**, not just for heart events.
- How much the **food source** (dairy vs. processed meat vs. butter) changes the picture.
- The ideal **omega-6 to omega-3 balance**.
- How fats relate to cancer, brain health, and metabolic outcomes, where the data is much thinner than for heart disease.

Most of the strongest evidence in this area is about cholesterol and cardiovascular disease. If you have high LDL, a family history of heart disease, or other health considerations, talk with a doctor or registered dietitian about the specifics.
